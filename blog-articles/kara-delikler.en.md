# Black Holes: The Wells of Spacetime

**Emoji:** 🕳️
**Tags:** astrophysics, black-hole, cosmology
**Summary:** How can regions exist from which not even light escapes? From the Schwarzschild radius to Hawking radiation, we examine the universe's most extreme objects.

## Escape Velocity and the Event Horizon

The speed you must reach to escape from a body's surface is called the **escape velocity**. On Earth it is about 11.2 km/s. But if you compress the same mass into a smaller volume, the escape velocity rises.

When an object is compressed to a small enough volume, the escape velocity **exceeds the speed of light**. From that point nothing — not even light — can get out. This boundary is called the **event horizon**, and its radius is the **Schwarzschild radius**:

`r_s = 2GM / c²`

This simple formula was found in 1916 by Karl Schwarzschild as an exact solution to Einstein's equations of general relativity.

## How Small Must the Mass Be?

The Schwarzschild radius is directly proportional to mass. Some examples:

- If the Sun's mass were a black hole, its radius would be only ~3 km.
- If Earth's mass were a black hole, its radius would be ~9 mm (a marble!).
- The supermassive black hole Sgr A* at the heart of the Milky Way has a mass of ~4 million Suns; its radius is about 12 million km (as wide as the Sun's orbit).

So a black hole is not a "monster that devours everything" — it is the region where **spacetime closes in on itself when mass no longer fits its volume**.

## Beyond the Event Horizon

What happens to an observer falling toward the event horizon? A distant observer sees the falling person's clocks slow down and their light shift into the red as they approach the horizon — but they can never quite watch them cross it. Because of time dilation, the falling image fades and disappears.

The falling person's own experience is different: tidal forces make the gravitational pull differ increasingly across their body. For small black holes this can be violent enough to tear a spaceship apart — "spaghettification".

## Hawking Radiation: Do Black Holes "Evaporate"?

In 1974, Stephen Hawking showed that quantum effects create pairs of particles near the event horizon. This radiation slowly lowers the black hole's mass. The Hawking temperature formula:

`T = ℏc³ / (8πGMk_B)`

An interesting detail: temperature is inversely proportional to mass. Stellar-mass black holes are extremely cold (a millionth of a kelvin). But a black hole losing mass (evaporating) gets hotter and hotter, and can eventually vanish in a burst of intense energy.

## Why Does It Matter?

- **Bekenstein entropy:** A black hole's entropy behaves as if it were "written" on its event horizon. This sits at the heart of quantum-gravity research.
- **Black hole mergers:** In 2015, LIGO first directly measured gravitational waves from two merging black holes.
- **Real images:** In 2019 the Event Horizon Telescope released the shadow of M87*'s black hole; in 2022 it released the image of Sgr A*.
